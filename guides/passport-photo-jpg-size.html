Photo dimension guide

Passport photo JPG size: pixels, DPI and print size

A passport photo is rejected for composition, dimensions or file size, usually in that order. Compression cannot fix a bad crop, but it can fix the byte count without destroying the face. This guide connects the three numbers so you can prepare the right file once.

Updated 14 November 20268 min readCalculator runs locally

Quick answer

A 2×2 inch photo at 300 DPI is 600×600 pixels. A 35×45 mm photo at 300 DPI is about 413×531 pixels. Digital passport systems often specify pixels directly, so convert from the print size only when the form asks for inches or millimetres. Always verify the current rule with the issuing authority before submitting.

Why passport photos fail even when they look fine

Passport and visa acceptance is a rule-matching problem. The reviewer or automated checker looks for a plain background, even lighting, a neutral expression, both ears visible or hair away from the face depending on the country, no glasses, and a head size within a specified range. Separately, the file must meet a pixel range, an aspect ratio and a byte cap. A beautiful portrait can fail every automated check if it is 500×700 instead of 600×600.

Compression enters the process late. First crop to the required aspect ratio, then resize to the required pixel dimensions, then encode to a JPEG under the byte cap. Doing it in the other order, compressing a full-body photo and then cropping, throws away quality twice and often leaves the head too small.

The DPI arithmetic

DPI means dots per inch. For a digital photo, it describes the intended print density, not the number of pixels. The relationship is simple: pixels = inches × DPI. A 2×2 inch photo at 300 DPI is 600×600 pixels. The same photo at 600 DPI is 1200×1200 pixels, with four times the pixel count but the same physical size when printed.

Metric photo sizes work the same way after converting millimetres to inches. A 35×45 mm photo is about 1.38×1.77 inches. At 300 DPI that is roughly 413×531 pixels. Photo labs often round to 413×531 or 420×540 depending on their template; the issuing authority’s stated pixel range is the safer target.

A JPEG’s DPI tag is metadata. Changing it from 72 to 300 does not add pixels and does not improve quality. It only changes the physical size a compliant printer would assign to the existing pixels. For a digital upload, treat the pixel dimensions as the real requirement and ignore the DPI tag unless the form explicitly asks for it.

File-size limits for passport uploads

Digital passport systems usually allow a generous byte range compared with job forms: often a few hundred kilobytes to 10MB. The US Department of State’s digital photo guidance, for example, describes a square JPEG between 600×600 and 1200×1200 pixels and a maximum file size of 10MB; check travel.state.gov for the current version. Other countries may ask for 20KB to 100KB, especially on older consular portals. The byte cap is usually the easiest constraint to satisfy once the pixels are correct.

Do not over-compress a passport photo to hit a low cap if the form allows more. Facial recognition and manual review both benefit from detail around the eyes, the hairline and the jaw. A 20KB image at 600×600 is about 0.46 bits per pixel and can look waxy. If the portal permits 500KB, use it.

Preparing the file in the right order

  1. Start from the original camera or phone photo. Do not use a screenshot of a photo, and do not re-use a compressed copy from an old application.
  2. Crop to the required aspect ratio. Square for a 2×2 inch rule, 35:45 for the common metric rule. Keep the head centred with the required space above it.
  3. Resize to the required pixel dimensions. Use a high-quality resampler. Avoid enlarging a small source; a 300×300 crop cannot become a credible 600×600 passport photo.
  4. Check the background and lighting. Plain, light and evenly lit. Remove shadows before encoding.
  5. Encode to JPEG at a quality that keeps the face clear. Use the highest quality that stays under the byte cap, not the lowest file size you can force.
  6. Verify the properties. Confirm width, height, format and exact byte count. Open the file at 100% and inspect the eyes and hairline.

What not to do

Do not edit the photo to change your appearance. Do not use beauty filters, heavy skin smoothing or generative fill; passport systems increasingly check for manipulation. Do not add a border to reach a dimension, and do not stretch a non-square photo into a square. A stretched face is an automatic rejection in most manual reviews. Do not upload a photo that contains another person, a phone shadow or a visible camera reflection.

Do not assume a previous acceptance means the same file will work again. Rules change, portals change their byte caps, and a file that passed last year may fail a new automated check. Re-read the current instructions for the specific document and country.

Head size and framing matter as much as dimensions

Passport systems often specify a head height range as a percentage of the image. A 600×600 photo with a tiny head in a large frame can fail even though the pixel count is correct. The usual guidance is to fill most of the frame with the head and shoulders, leaving a small margin above the hair and below the chin. Exact percentages vary by country, so check the current instructions rather than copying a template from another document.

Framing also affects compression. A face that occupies more pixels carries more detail in the same byte budget. If you are fighting a low cap, crop closer before reducing quality. Do not crop so close that the top of the head or the chin is cut; that is an automatic rejection in most systems.

Printing at home versus a photo lab

If you print at home, the printer driver may resample the image to its own resolution and change the effective DPI. A 600×600 file printed at 2×2 inches is 300 DPI, which is a common photo-lab standard. A 1200×1200 file printed at the same size is 600 DPI, which most consumer printers cannot fully use but which gives the lab more room to crop. Ask the lab what pixel size it expects; many labs publish a template with exact dimensions.

Do not add a white border to reach a paper size. The passport photo must be cut to the required dimensions, and a border changes the head-to-frame ratio. Print on photo paper with a matte or glossy finish that the authority accepts, and avoid home printers that leave visible banding across the face.

Frequently asked questions

Does converting to JPG change the print size?

No. The pixel dimensions stay the same unless you resize. JPEG compression changes how precisely each pixel is stored, not how many pixels exist. The DPI tag may change, which affects the nominal print size but not the image content.

Can I use a phone photo for a passport application?

Often yes, if the resolution is high enough and the lighting is even. Phone portraits frequently have a blurred background or a slight wide-angle distortion. Check the head size and background rules, and avoid digital zoom, which reduces real resolution.

Why is my 600×600 photo rejected as too small?

The authority may require 600×600 as a minimum and 1200×1200 as a maximum, but it can also require a minimum file size or a particular head-to-frame ratio. A 600×600 image with a tiny head inside a large empty frame fails the composition rule even though the dimensions are correct.
