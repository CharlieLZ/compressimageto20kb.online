Quality guide

Reduce JPG size without losing visible quality

“Without losing quality” is impossible in the literal sense: JPEG is lossy, so every encode removes something. The useful goal is to remove what the viewer will not notice at the size the image will actually be seen. That goal is achievable with a consistent order of operations.

Updated 14 November 20268 min readTool runs locally

Quick answer

Crop away what the image does not need, resize to the display size, then encode once at the highest quality that meets the byte target. Never stack multiple compression generations. For screen viewing, reducing dimensions usually preserves perceived sharpness better than pushing quality down on a large canvas.

Define “quality” before you compress

Quality is not a single property. It includes sharpness, colour accuracy, tonal smoothness, freedom from blocking and the absence of ringing around edges. Different viewers weight those differently. A photographer may care about shadow detail; a recruiter cares that the face is clear; a shopper cares that the product colour is believable. Compression decisions should follow the use, not an abstract score.

This matters because a smaller file can look better at the intended size. A 4000×3000 photo displayed at 600×450 has more pixels than the screen can show. Resizing it to 800×600 and encoding at high quality produces a smaller and often sharper-looking result than keeping 4000 pixels and crushing the quality. The extra pixels were never visible; the lower quality is.

The order that preserves the most detail

  1. Start from the best source. The camera original or the highest-quality export. Never begin with a file that has already been compressed for a previous upload.
  2. Crop to the final composition. Remove empty borders, irrelevant background and anything the destination does not need. Cropping before resizing gives the subject more of the remaining pixels.
  3. Resize once to the display size. Use a high-quality resampler. If the destination is a 600-pixel preview, do not keep 4000 pixels “just in case”; keep the original separately and make a delivery copy.
  4. Apply minimal sharpening if needed. Downscaling softens edges slightly. A small unsharp mask can restore perceived crispness, but too much adds noise and bytes.
  5. Encode once to the target. Let the compressor search quality and dimensions together. Save the final JPEG and do not re-open and re-save it for minor changes.
  6. Inspect at 100%. Check eyes, text, hair and product edges. If they fail, go back to the source and adjust the crop or dimensions rather than re-compressing the output.

Why repeated compression is the real enemy

Each JPEG encode quantises the image again. Artefacts from the first pass become part of the input for the second, so the encoder spends bits representing blocks, ringing and colour noise that were not in the original scene. The result is often larger than expected and visibly worse. A file compressed to 100KB, then re-compressed to 50KB, is not equivalent to a single 50KB encode from the original.

The same logic applies to editing. Opening a JPEG, changing the brightness, saving, reopening and cropping creates multiple generations. If you expect to edit, work in a lossless format and export to JPEG once at the end. If you only have the JPEG, do all edits in one session and save once.

One source, one crop, one resize, one encode. Everything else is a generation you did not need.

Dimensions versus quality, in visual terms

Lowering JPEG quality affects every 8×8 block. At moderate settings, the loss appears as slightly softer texture and faint blocking in smooth gradients. At aggressive settings, edges develop halos, fine lines disappear and flat areas show visible squares. Reducing dimensions, by contrast, produces a smaller but internally consistent image. When viewed at its intended size, it can look sharper because each remaining pixel is encoded with more bits.

The trade-off reverses when the destination displays the image large. A 400-pixel image stretched to a 1200-pixel hero will look soft regardless of its JPEG quality. In that case, keep enough pixels and accept a larger file, or use a format and delivery method designed for the size.

Reading artefacts: what to fix and what to accept

  • Blocking in smooth gradients: the quality is too low for the tonal range. Reduce dimensions or increase the target.
  • Ringing around text and edges: JPEG’s block transform struggling with sharp transitions. Use PNG for text, or a higher quality.
  • Colour smearing: chroma subsampling discards colour detail. Usually acceptable for photos, noticeable on saturated logos and thin coloured lines.
  • Mosquito noise around high-contrast edges: small speckles near dark lines. Lower quality makes it worse; a mild denoise before encoding can help.
  • Banding in skies: smooth gradients run out of tonal steps. Slightly higher quality or a subtle dither before encoding can reduce it.
  • Soft faces: the most important failure for portraits. Crop closer and resize rather than crushing quality further.

When to stop optimising

Stop when the image passes the destination’s rules and looks correct at the size it will be viewed. Chasing the last 10KB usually costs visible quality for no practical benefit. A 98KB file that looks clean is better than an 80KB file with a waxy face, even if both pass the portal. The purpose of compression is delivery, not a smaller number for its own sake.

Keep the original and the delivery copy separate. Name them clearly, for example portrait-original.jpg and portrait-600w.jpg. When a future portal asks for a different size, start from the original again.

A note on perceived sharpness

Human vision is more sensitive to edges than to fine texture. That is why a slightly downscaled image with crisp edges can look sharper than a full-size image with mushy edges, even when the full-size file is larger. When you compare two outputs, view them at the same physical size on screen. Comparing a 4000-pixel file at 100% with a 600-pixel file at 100% is not a fair test; the first is magnified far beyond its display size.

This also explains why aggressive sharpening before compression can backfire. The sharpener adds high-frequency detail, the encoder spends bytes preserving it, and the result is a larger file with more visible ringing. Apply sharpening sparingly, after resizing, and judge the final file rather than the editing preview.

Frequently asked questions

Is quality 80 always safe?

No. A quality number means different things at different dimensions and for different content. Quality 80 at 600×600 may look clean; at 3000×2000 it may still be a large file, and at 200×200 it may be unnecessary. Judge the output, not the number.

Does converting to grayscale reduce size?

Yes, because only one channel is encoded. It is a legitimate choice for documents and some portraits, but it changes the image. Do not do it unless the destination allows or wants monochrome.

Can I improve a blurry compressed JPG?

Not really. Sharpening can increase perceived edge contrast, but it cannot restore detail that was discarded. Start from a better source if the image matters.
