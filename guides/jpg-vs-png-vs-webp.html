JPG vs PNG vs WebP: which format for which image

Format choice is a decision about what the image contains. Photographs, flat interface screenshots and images with transparency have different needs. Choosing the wrong container can double the file size or introduce visible damage that no quality slider can fix.

Updated 14 November 20267 min readTool runs locally

Quick answer

Use JPEG for photographs and anything with smooth tonal gradation. Use PNG for text, line art, screenshots and images that need transparency or lossless editing. Use WebP for web delivery when you can serve a fallback; it often beats JPEG on size but needs a modern browser. Never convert a JPEG to PNG expecting better quality.

The one real difference: lossy versus lossless

JPEG is a lossy format. It transforms blocks of pixels into frequency coefficients, quantises them and discards information the eye is supposedly less sensitive to. Every encode permanently removes detail. PNG is lossless: the decoded image matches the encoded image exactly. That makes PNG larger for photographs but ideal for text, diagrams and screenshots where exact pixels matter.

WebP supports both modes. A lossy WebP behaves like JPEG but often compresses better at the same visual quality. A lossless WebP behaves like PNG and can be smaller. The catch is compatibility and workflow: older software, some email clients and a few upload portals do not accept WebP even when browsers can display it.

Why JPEG is still the default for photographs

JPEG has three advantages that matter beyond compression ratio: universal support, predictable behaviour and a mature ecosystem. Every browser, phone, printer, email client and upload portal accepts it. Camera pipelines produce it natively. The format’s weaknesses, blocking and ringing at low quality, are well understood, so a careful encoder can avoid the worst of them.

JPEG is also good at exactly what photographs contain: smooth gradients, skin tones and natural texture. The transform works on 8×8 blocks and spends most bits on luminance, which matches human vision. At a reasonable quality, a photograph looks continuous even though most of the high-frequency detail is gone.

Where JPEG fails

JPEG fails on hard edges, flat colour and text. A screenshot of a document has black letters on white, with sharp transitions that the block transform cannot represent cheaply. The encoder either blurs the letters or creates ringing halos around them. Increasing quality helps but also increases the file size, often beyond the equivalent PNG. A screenshot saved as JPEG at 20KB is usually worse than the same screenshot as PNG at 40KB.

JPEG also lacks transparency. If a logo has a transparent background and you save it as JPEG, the transparent area becomes white or black depending on the encoder. The result may look acceptable on one page and wrong on another. Use PNG or WebP when transparency is part of the asset.

WebP: useful, but read the requirement

WebP generally produces smaller files than JPEG at similar visual quality for photographs, and smaller files than PNG for lossless graphics. Browser support is broad in current versions of Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari. That makes it a strong choice for website assets where you control the markup and can provide a fallback.

Do not assume every destination accepts it. Many upload forms whitelist only .jpg, .jpeg and .png. Email clients may display a WebP inline but fail to attach it as expected. Older design tools may not open it. If a portal asks for JPG, deliver JPG; converting a WebP to JPEG is a lossy step and should be done from the best available source.

Test the format the destination requires, not the format your browser prefers. Compatibility failures are more expensive than a few kilobytes saved.

Converting between formats without making things worse

Conversion direction matters. Converting a lossless PNG to JPEG is a one-way lossy step: you gain a smaller file and permanently lose exact pixels. Converting a JPEG to PNG is usually pointless: the file gets larger, and the missing detail does not return. Converting a JPEG to WebP is another lossy generation unless you use lossless WebP, which may be larger than the original JPEG.

The safe workflow is to keep an original in the best available format and generate delivery copies from it. For a photograph, that means the camera original or a high-quality export. For a logo, the vector source. For a screenshot, a fresh capture. Re-encoding a delivery copy repeatedly accumulates artefacts and may not even reduce size after the first pass.

What to check before uploading

  • Allowed extensions: read the form. If it says JPG only, do not upload WebP with a renamed extension.
  • Transparency: if the image needs it, JPEG is the wrong format.
  • Text legibility: zoom to 100%. If letters are fuzzy, use PNG or a higher JPEG quality.
  • File size: compare the actual bytes, not the format’s reputation.
  • Colour profile: some portals expect sRGB. A wide-gamut image may shift colours after upload.
  • Metadata: re-encoding strips EXIF, which is useful for privacy but may remove orientation if the source relies on it. Modern browsers apply orientation before drawing; verify the result is upright.

Colour profiles and transparency traps

A JPEG can carry an ICC colour profile, and a browser or printer may interpret it differently from the original. A wide-gamut photo converted to sRGB can look slightly duller but more consistent across devices. For uploads, sRGB is the safest assumption unless the destination specifies otherwise. Strip or convert unusual profiles before submitting, and compare the result on a neutral background.

Transparency is the other common trap. Saving a transparent PNG as JPEG fills the transparent area with a solid colour chosen by the encoder, often white or black. If the logo is placed on a coloured header, the filled rectangle becomes visible. Keep transparency in PNG or WebP, or place the logo on the exact background colour before exporting to JPEG.

A simple decision path

If the image is a photograph and the destination accepts JPEG, use JPEG. If it contains text, flat colour or transparency, use PNG unless the destination forbids it. If you control a website and need smaller delivery, test WebP with a JPEG fallback. If the destination names a format, that requirement overrides every general preference. The rest is measurement: compare the actual bytes and inspect the actual pixels rather than trusting a format’s reputation.

Frequently asked questions

Is PNG higher quality than JPG?

PNG is lossless, so it preserves exact pixels. That is higher fidelity, not automatically a better-looking image. A photograph saved as PNG is larger and looks the same as a high-quality JPEG; a JPEG that has already lost detail cannot be restored by converting to PNG.

Can I use WebP on a website today?

Yes, with a fallback for older clients and a check that your CDN serves the right content type. For a simple static site, JPEG remains the lowest-risk choice for photographs.

Why did my PNG get larger when I converted it to JPG?

That is unusual for a photograph but common for a screenshot with large flat areas and sharp text, where PNG’s lossless prediction is very efficient. It can also happen if the JPEG is saved at a very high quality setting.
